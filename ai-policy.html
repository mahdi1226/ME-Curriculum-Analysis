ABET's AI Policy

In 2025, ABET released its official position on the use of artificial intelligence in accredited engineering programs. The policy reflects a balanced approach that acknowledges AI's growing role while preserving the centrality of human expertise.

  • ABET will not prescribe how programs use AI in teaching or operations
  • Programs may use AI tools for accreditation preparation and documentation
  • All AI-generated materials must be verified by qualified personnel before submission
  • Accreditation commissions may add AI-related curricular topics on a discipline-by-discipline basis
  • The Engineering Accreditation Commission (EAC) has proposed Criterion 5 curriculum changes for the 2026-2027 cycle
  • New AI/ML program criteria were approved for computing programs in October 2025
ABET's Key Principle:

"AI should support and enhance human judgment, not replace it."

Ethics in the AI Era — Deep Dive

Modern mechanical engineering ethics must address challenges that did not exist a decade ago. The following topics represent essential areas for coverage in an updated engineering ethics curriculum.

1. AI Decision-Making Liability

When an AI-designed component fails — a bracket optimized by generative design, a control system tuned by ML — the question of liability becomes complex. Traditional engineering liability assumes a human designer made decisions; AI disrupts this chain of responsibility. Students must learn to navigate shared liability between engineers, software developers, and organizations.

Discussion Question

A bridge support bracket was designed using AI-driven topology optimization and later fails under load. The engineer approved the design, the software company provided the tool, and the client specified the constraints. Who bears responsibility, and how should liability be allocated?

2. Algorithmic Bias

AI systems trained on historical data can perpetuate and amplify existing biases. In engineering, this manifests in designs optimized for majority populations — such as crash test simulations calibrated primarily to male body dimensions, or HVAC systems designed around narrow occupant profiles. Engineers must learn to audit AI outputs for representational fairness.

Discussion Question

Your company's AI design tool consistently produces vehicle safety features optimized for a specific demographic. How would you identify this bias, and what steps should an engineer take to ensure equitable design outcomes?

3. Autonomous Systems

Self-driving vehicles, autonomous manufacturing robots, and delivery drones all involve mechanical systems making real-time decisions without human intervention. Engineers must grapple with how to design fail-safes, define acceptable risk thresholds, and establish human override mechanisms.

Discussion Question

You are designing the mechanical fail-safe system for an autonomous warehouse robot. Under what conditions should the system override AI control, and how do you balance safety with operational efficiency?

4. Data Privacy

IoT-enabled mechanical systems — smart HVAC, connected vehicles, wearable devices — continuously collect data about users and environments. Engineers designing these systems must consider what data is collected, how it is stored, who has access, and what happens when systems are compromised.

Discussion Question

A smart building HVAC system you designed collects occupancy patterns, temperature preferences, and movement data. A third party requests access to this data for "energy research." What ethical obligations do you have, and what safeguards should be in place?

5. Environmental Impact

Training large AI models consumes substantial energy and water resources. Engineers must weigh the computational cost of AI-assisted design against the sustainability benefits the resulting designs may provide. This includes considering the full lifecycle environmental impact of AI-integrated products.

Discussion Question

Your team wants to use a large AI model to optimize a product for energy efficiency, but the training process itself has a significant carbon footprint. How do you evaluate whether the environmental cost of the AI is justified by the gains in the final product?

6. Professional Responsibility

Engineers have a professional obligation to verify the correctness and safety of their work. When AI generates designs, simulations, or analyses, the engineer who signs off remains responsible. Blind trust in AI outputs is a violation of professional duty. Students must learn when and how to validate AI-generated engineering work.

Discussion Question

An AI tool generates a structural analysis that passes all automated checks, but you notice an assumption in the model that seems physically unreasonable. Your deadline is tomorrow. What do you do, and what does your professional code of ethics require?

7. Intellectual Property and Attribution

AI-generated designs raise unresolved questions about copyright, patent eligibility, and professional attribution. If an AI tool generates a novel mechanism, can it be patented? Who owns the design — the engineer, the company, or the tool provider? Professional engineering standards require clear attribution practices that current AI workflows complicate.

Discussion Question

You use an AI tool to generate a novel heat exchanger geometry that significantly outperforms existing designs. Your company wants to patent it. Who should be listed as the inventor, and what disclosure obligations do you have regarding the AI's role?

ASEE Research on AI in ME Education

Recent papers presented at ASEE (American Society for Engineering Education) conferences highlight consistent themes around AI integration in mechanical engineering curricula:

  • AI ethics coverage is often absent: Students report that existing curricula rarely address the ethical dimensions of AI use in engineering. Most ethics courses have not been updated to reflect AI-related dilemmas.
  • Hands-on applications drive engagement: Courses that incorporate lab exercises and practical AI applications (e.g., ML for sensor data, computer vision for inspection) report significantly higher student interest and retention compared to lecture-only approaches.
  • Faculty training is essential: Many faculty members lack experience with AI tools and require professional development before they can effectively integrate AI content into their courses. Institutions that invest in faculty training see better outcomes.
  • Assessment requires rethinking: AI-generated student work creates challenges for traditional assessment methods. Programs are exploring new approaches including process documentation, oral examinations, and AI-use reflections.

Implementation Roadmap

A phased approach ensures manageable adoption without overwhelming faculty or students. Each phase builds on the previous one.

Phase Actions Timeline
Phase 1 Update PHIL 2259 (Engineering Ethics) with 3-4 weeks of AI content. Modernize CMP SCI 1250 (Intro to Computing) to Python-first with AI tool coverage. Year 1
Phase 2 Add AI modules to MENG 3350 (System Dynamics & Control), MENG 3360 (Machine Design & Manufacturing), and MENG 4490 (Advanced Manufacturing). Year 2
Phase 3 Launch MENG 4XXX (AI/ML for Mechanical Engineers) and MENG 3XXX (Mechatronics and Smart Systems) as elective offerings. Year 3
Phase 4 Full integration across all courses. Assess impact on student outcomes and ABET criteria. Iterate based on data and evolving standards. Year 4
