About the DEEP Project

Citation: Busch-Vishniac, I. et al. (2011). "Deconstructing Engineering Education Programmes: The DEEP Project to reform the mechanical engineering curriculum." European Journal of Engineering Education, 36(3), 269–283. DOI: 10.1080/03043797.2011.579590

The DEEP project aimed to revise the standard ME undergraduate curriculum to attract and retain a more diverse community of students. The team included faculty from 8 universities (Cal State LA, Michigan State, Howard, Tuskegee, Stevens Institute, U. of Washington, Smith College, and Johns Hopkins) plus MIT as a reference.

Methodology

  1. Dissect syllabi from partner institutions into individual topics — produced 833 unique topics
  2. Define prerequisite relationships between topics (not courses) — topic-level granularity
  3. Apply genetic algorithm to cluster topics into groups that minimize cross-cluster dependencies
  4. Result: 12 clusters (A–L), each roughly equivalent to a full-year course (~84 instruction hours)

The key insight: by working at the topic level rather than the course level, prerequisite chains can be shortened significantly, giving students more scheduling flexibility.

Key Findings

Low Standardization

Of ~1,000 topics across 9 programs, only 70 were common to the majority. ME programs are far less standardized than assumed. The body of knowledge considered necessary is actually small enough to fit into a four-year program.

Reduced Dependencies

The genetic algorithm reduced cross-cluster dependencies from 988 to 334 links (158 prerequisite + 176 successor cross-topic links). This is a 66% reduction in inter-course coupling.

Mixed Subdiscipline Clusters

The 12 clusters mix topics from different traditional subdisciplines (e.g., thermodynamics + kinematics in the same cluster). This is a fundamental departure from traditional course organization where each course = one subdiscipline.

Entry Flexibility

Setting a maximum of 7 cross-cluster links gives enough flexibility for a 4-year program with Year 2 entry possible. At 8 links max, students entering a year late could finish in 3 engineering-intensive years.

UMSL Critical Paths

The Year 2 Spring Problem

Year 2 Spring is the critical semester. It contains 4 gateway courses (Dynamics, Mechanics of Materials, Thermo, Diff Eq) that collectively gate almost ALL of Year 3 and Year 4. A student who fails ANY of these courses cannot take most Year 3 courses and will be delayed by a full year.

Compare: Year 2 Spring has 15 credits of tightly coupled prerequisites. Year 3 Spring has 18 credits, the heaviest semester — all depending on Year 2 Spring success.

Recommendations Based on DEEP

1. Reduce Year 3 Spring Overload

Move MENG 3390 (Product Dev Lab, 1 cr) and/or MENG 3340 (Materials, 3 cr) to Year 3 Fall to balance the load. Materials only requires ENGR 2332, which is available after Y2 Spring.

2. Create Parallel Tracks

Allow thermal-track and mechanical-track students to take upper-level courses in different orders. Currently, all students must take the same fixed sequence. Elective slots would enable this naturally.

3. Add Elective Flexibility

Converting fixed Year 4 courses (4440, 4450, 4460) into elective choices reduces path rigidity. Students who struggle with one area can choose alternatives without delay.

4. Consider Topic-Level Reorganization

Some UMSL courses already blend subdisciplines — MENG 3360 combines Machine Design AND Manufacturing. This is DEEP-aligned. Consider more blended courses that teach related topics together, just-in-time.

5. Strengthen Year 2 Spring Support

Since Y2 Spring is the critical gateway, consider: supplemental instruction, tutoring programs, early-warning systems for students struggling in Statics (Y2 Fall), and summer course offerings for Dynamics/Thermo to allow recovery.

6. Offer Summer Critical Courses

Offering ENGR 2320 (Dynamics), ENGR 2330 (Thermo), or MATH 2020 (Diff Eq) in summer would give students who failed in Spring a path to re-enter the sequence in Fall without a full-year delay. This is the single most impactful retention intervention.

Traditional vs. DEEP-Inspired

Traditional Structure

Linear chains, one subject per course, rigid sequencing

Statics→ Dynamics→ Mechanisms→ Machine Design→ Capstone
Thermo→ Fluids→ Heat Transfer→ CFD
  • Each course = one subdiscipline
  • Long sequential chains
  • Fail one course = semester+ delay
  • Little schedule flexibility
  • Students enter in Year 1 or not at all

Practical Consideration

DEEP represents an ideal restructuring. Full implementation would require rewriting textbooks and retraining faculty. However, UMSL can apply DEEP principles within its existing structure:

  • Blend subdisciplines where natural (already done with MENG 3360)
  • Add elective flexibility to break rigid chains
  • Offer summer courses at bottleneck points
  • Move courses earlier when prerequisite chains allow
